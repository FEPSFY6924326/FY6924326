Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) depends heavily on mathematical ideas such as algebra, probability and optimisation. This article explains how maths forms the structure behind modern AI systems and how these systems are now being used in medicine. Examples include medical image analysis, drug discovery and the growing use of “digital twins” to model individual patients. These developments could lead to earlier diagnosis and more personalised healthcare for future generations. However, issues such as data privacy, bias and uneven access to technology may also create challenges. The aim of this paper is to give a clear overview of how maths supports AI in medical settings and to consider how these changes may affect the younger generations who will grow up alongside these technologies.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming a normal part of modern life, appearing in phones, online services, transport systems and hospitals. Even though AI seems complicated, its foundations are mathematical. Computers learn patterns using equations, probability and optimisation methods that allow them to improve over time. Because of this, mathematics plays a central role in how reliable and useful AI systems can be. It is important for students to understand this connection because AI is likely to influence many careers, especially in healthcare, engineering and computing. AI systems are already helping doctors analyse images and detect diseases, which can save time and reduce errors. At the same time, AI could affect future generations in both positive and negative ways. Young people may benefit from better medical predictions, but they might also face issues such as privacy problems or reduced control over automated decisions. This article explains how maths supports AI, how AI is being used in medicine, and what future generations may experience as these tools continue to develop.

Analysis and discussion

Mathematics plays a key part in how AI works, especially in systems used for medical tasks. One of the main areas is linear algebra, which represents data as vectors and matrices. Medical images, for example, are stored as grids of numbers, with each number showing pixel brightness. Neural networks use repeated matrix operations to recognise patterns in these images (Goodfellow et al., 2016). These patterns help the AI identify whether something in the image looks normal or unusual. Probability and statistics are also essential. Real medical data is rarely perfect, and AI must deal with uncertainty. A system might predict that a patient has a 60% chance of a particular condition based on their scan. Doctors can then use statistical measures like accuracy and sensitivity to judge how trustworthy the model is. These ideas help make AI more reliable in real-life situations, where decisions cannot be based on guesses alone. Optimisation, which uses calculus, allows AI models to improve during training. By comparing predictions with correct answers, the model calculates an error value and then adjusts itself to reduce this error. Although the maths can get complicated, the basic idea is similar to reducing a quadratic function to find its minimum. This supports the learning process that makes AI systems more accurate over time. In medicine, the impact of mathematically based AI is already visible. A well-known example is a system that classifies skin lesions. Researchers found that their AI model could reach a performance level similar to specialist dermatologists (Esteva et al., 2017). This means AI can help doctors make faster decisions, especially in places where experienced staff may not be available. Another major development is AlphaFold, which predicts protein structures using mathematical methods involving geometry and probability (Jumper et al., 2021). Understanding protein shapes can speed up drug development and help create new treatments. More recently, the idea of a “digital twin” has emerged. A digital twin is a virtual model of an individual patient created using maths, AI and medical data. It can be used to test different treatments before they are tried on the real patient. This approach has been studied particularly in heart medicine (Corral-Acero et al., 2020). Digital twins could become more common for future generations, giving people more personalised and safer healthcare. While the exact values vary, the trend is that more advanced AI methods often lead to greater improvements in diagnosis and treatment.

As future generations grow up with these technologies, they may experience earlier diagnosis, personalised treatment plans and more efficient healthcare systems. However, these benefits come with challenges. Biased data can lead to unfair results, and younger people may have to develop stronger digital skills to understand how AI systems make decisions. Schools and universities might need to adapt, ensuring students learn both the technical and ethical sides of AI. Overall, the effects of mathematical AI will depend on how responsibly it is used and how well society prepares for these changes.
