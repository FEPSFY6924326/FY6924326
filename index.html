Abstract

This paper explores the connection between mathematics and artificial intelligence, with a particular focus 
on how these ideas are shaping modern medicine. Much of today’s AI relies on mathematical structures—such 
as optimisation algorithms and probability models—and although these are rarely visible to patients, they form 
the backbone of diagnostic tools and treatment-support systems. The paper introduces key mathematical 
concepts used in AI and then applies them to a simplified medical screening scenario. By examining different 
AI screening options with varying costs and detection rates, the analysis shows how mathematical reasoning 
can clarify when AI improves long-term outcomes and when it might introduce new risks. This is increasingly 
important for future generations as AI becomes more embedded in healthcare.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has accelerated so quickly that it is easy to forget how dependent it still is on quite 
traditional areas of mathematics. Even systems that appear “intelligent” on the surface—like tumour-detection 
networks or diagnostic chatbots—are powered underneath by probability, linear algebra, optimisation, and 
geometry (Esteva et al., 2019). These ideas do not disappear just because AI interfaces look simple; they 
remain essential for checking accuracy, fairness and reliability, especially in something as sensitive as medical 
decision-making (Topol, 2019).

Medical imaging is often cited as one of the biggest success stories in AI-assisted healthcare. Early image 
processing techniques used Fourier transforms or edge filters—very strict mathematical tools. But modern AI 
systems learn flexible mathematical representations using gradient descent and high-dimensional spaces. 
Hospitals trial these tools because they can sometimes detect tumours earlier than humans, although their 
performance varies with scanner quality and training data (Crossnohere et al., 2022). This is where statistics 
matters: doctors need to know how confident the AI is, and how often it tends to make borderline mistakes.

Another growing area is personalised medicine, which uses patient-specific data such as genetics or lifestyle 
patterns. AI helps interpret extremely large datasets using clustering and dimensionality-reduction techniques, 
but these only work well when the mathematical foundations are stable. Otherwise, the “patterns” found by 
the model may simply reflect noise or bias (Rajpurkar et al., 2022). This is a real concern in healthcare where 
unreliable predictions can lead to poor treatment choices.

As AI becomes more common, students and future engineers will need a better understanding of the maths 
behind it—not just how to run models but how to question them. Knowing even basic statistics helps people 
spot unusual behaviour, like an AI giving overconfident predictions on low-quality scans. The next section 
develops these ideas through a more concrete medical screening example.

Analysis and discussion

To understand how maths and AI interact in real healthcare settings, it is useful to consider a simplified but 
realistic example. Hospitals choosing between different cancer-screening tools face a trade-off: cheaper 
options are more affordable but less accurate, while advanced AI systems can detect early-stage disease more 
effectively but may be too expensive for smaller clinics to adopt (Alowais et al., 2023).

Imagine a hospital comparing five AI-assisted screening systems. The cheapest system uses a small neural 
network model and misses many early cases. A mid-range system achieves better sensitivity because it is trained 
on a more diverse dataset. The most advanced option uses a deep learning model that performs extremely well, 
but its cost is so high that it could strain hospital budgets. Table 1 presents a simplified set of figures showing 
how annual cost correlates with early-detection performance.

If a hospital looks only at the upfront cost, the cheapest tool seems the obvious winner. But once we use even 
a basic mathematical model—including probabilities of missed diagnoses, expected long-term treatment costs 
and population risk—the picture changes. Missing early-stage cancers leads to far greater costs later on, both 
financially and in terms of patient outcomes (Chustecki, 2024). Surprisingly, a mid-range AI system may offer 
the best long-term value even though it appears expensive at first glance.

This is where mathematics ties everything together. AI supplies risk predictions, but mathematical reasoning 
helps convert predictions into decisions. Tools such as probability distributions, optimisation models and even 
simple algebra can help predict long-term trends and compare strategies fairly (He et al., 2019). AI alone 
cannot judge which option is best; it simply outputs probabilities. The decision comes from combining these 
probabilities with realistic cost models.

For future generations, the key challenge is not just using AI but understanding its limitations. Medical data is 
often messy—full of missing values, measurement errors or unconscious biases. A student who understands 
basic statistics will recognise when an AI model seems “too confident” or is behaving strangely on new 
patients. AI is unlikely to replace human judgement entirely, but it can strengthen it when both mathematical 
thinking and ethical awareness are applied together.
