Abstract

This paper discusses the relationship between mathematics and artificial intelligence, with a particular focus
on how these tools are being used, and could be used in the future, within medicine. Modern AI systems rely
heavily on mathematical ideas such as optimisation, probability and high-dimensional geometry, yet these
links are often hidden from patients and sometimes even from practitioners. After introducing the basic
concepts of mathematical modelling in AI, the paper considers a simple case study related to medical
screening and treatment decisions. The analysis highlights how a combination of mathematical thinking
and AI tools can support more informed choices, but also shows that there are ethical and practical limits
which future generations will need to handle carefully.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has developed so quickly in recent years that it is now difficult to separate the
technology from the mathematics that underpins almost every aspect of it. Modern AI systems, including
neural networks and large language models, rely on mathematical concepts such as optimisation, probability
theory, high-dimensional geometry and statistical learning. While these ideas are often hidden behind user-
friendly interfaces, they remain essential for explaining why AI systems behave the way they do and, more
importantly, how they can be improved in a safe way. The connection between mathematics and AI is
particularly visible in medicine, where decisions must be based on reliable evidence and where errors can
have serious consequences for real patients.

One of the clearest examples of this link is in medical imaging. Classical image-processing already relied on
mathematical transforms and differential equations, but AI systems now use vector representations,
gradient-based learning and non-linear optimisation to detect tumours, classify tissue abnormalities and
estimate disease progression. Many hospitals are already testing these tools and, although the accuracy still
varies between different scanners and patient groups, the long-term potential is significant. Future
generations of doctors may depend heavily on such systems as populations grow older and the demand for
rapid diagnosis increases. Mathematics, particularly statistics, is central in making sure that AI does not
simply identify patterns blindly but instead quantifies uncertainty, detects bias and measures the confidence
of its predictions.

Another important area is personalised medicine, which aims to tailor treatment based on a patient’s genetic,
physiological or environmental data. The datasets in this area are huge, often containing thousands of
variables but relatively few patients. AI can extract structure from these data using dimensionality-reduction
techniques and clustering algorithms, but the stability of these patterns depends on the underlying linear
algebra and optimisation methods. If the mathematics is poorly understood or misapplied then treatment
decisions could be based on unstable relationships that disappear when new data are added.

For younger generations growing up surrounded by AI systems, mathematical literacy may therefore become
almost as important as basic digital literacy. Understanding the mathematical structures behind learning
algorithms will allow future engineers and medical researchers to design safer systems and to question model
outputs instead of accepting them as mysterious black-box answers. In the rest of this paper we discuss how
these ideas might change the future of medical decision making and what challenges still remain.

Analysis and discussion

The potential benefits of combining mathematics, artificial intelligence and medicine are quite broad, but they
are not automatic. A simple example, based on a small data set for the cost and lifespan of different medical
treatment strategies, helps to illustrate both the opportunities and some of the risks. In many health systems
there is pressure to choose the cheapest short-term option, for example a basic screening programme with
limited follow-up. If we only compare initial cost, this option appears attractive, in the same way that a cheap
repair to a bridge deck can look attractive in civil engineering. However, a more mathematical analysis that
includes expected lifespan, probability of failure and long-term cost can lead to a different conclusion.

Suppose a hospital can choose between several AI-supported screening strategies. A very cheap option might
use a small model that is fast but not very sensitive, so early-stage cancers are sometimes missed and patients
return later needing more intensive treatment. A more expensive option might use a larger neural network
trained on a wider range of images. This model requires more computing power and specialist staff but it can
detect subtle patterns that are not obvious to human experts. Using basic ideas from probability and decision
theory, engineers can estimate the expected number of correctly detected cases, the number of false alarms and
the downstream costs for each strategy over a period of years.

When these quantities are written into even a simple mathematical model, it often turns out that the mid-range
or more expensive option gives a lower cost per successful diagnosis, once future treatment and social costs
are included. This kind of reasoning is similar to life-cycle costing in structural engineering, but here the
objects of interest are patients rather than bridges. The role of AI is to provide accurate risk estimates based
on high-dimensional data, while mathematics glues together the probabilities, costs and time horizons into a
coherent decision framework.

For future generations, a key challenge will be making these models understandable to the people who rely on
them. Doctors and patients need explanations that connect mathematical quantities, such as prediction
intervals or gradient norms, to everyday ideas of safety and fairness. At the same time, university students in
engineering, mathematics and computer science will need some exposure to real medical problems so that they
do not design algorithms in isolation from clinical reality. A model that looks elegant on paper, or even performs
well on a narrow test set, can behave very differently when exposed to messy hospital data with missing values
and measurement errors.

Finally, there is an ethical dimension that simple equations cannot resolve on their own. AI-driven tools will
almost certainly play a large role in the medicine of the future, from automated triage systems to personalised
drug design. Mathematics can help to make their behaviour transparent and to set quantitative limits on error
rates, but society still has to decide how responsibility is shared between algorithms and humans. Accepting
that these systems will never be perfect, and allowing some room for human judgement and even a few
inefficiencies, may actually be healthier than pretending that AI can provide a single optimal answer for every
patient. This slightly untidy balance between rigorous mathematics and real-world constraints is likely to shape
how future generations experience medical care.
