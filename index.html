Abstract

This paper discusses the relationship between mathematics and artificial intelligence, with a particular focus
on how these tools are being used, and could be used in the future, within medicine. Modern AI systems rely
heavily on mathematical ideas such as optimisation, probability and high-dimensional geometry, yet these
links are often hidden from patients and even from some practitioners. After introducing the basic concepts of
mathematical modelling in AI, the paper considers a simple example related to medical screening decisions.
The analysis highlights how a mixture of mathematical reasoning and AI tools can support more informed
choices, but also shows that there are ethical and practical limits which future generations will need to handle
carefully.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has moved so quickly into everyday life that it can sometimes feel as if the maths
behind it has been forgotten or pushed out of sight. But the truth is that almost every AI system we use today,
whether it’s a medical image classifier or a chat-based model, still depends heavily on mathematical ideas.
Things like optimisation, probability, geometry and linear algebra are constantly running in the background,
even if most people (including many users in healthcare) never see them directly. This connection becomes
especially important when we look at how AI is starting to influence medical practice, because medical
decisions rely on evidence, reliability and fairness – and mathematics is what allows us to check those things.

A clear example of this is medical imaging, which has become one of the main “success stories’’ of AI.
Traditional medical image analysis relied on things like Fourier transforms or edge-detection filters, which are
very mathematical but fairly rigid. In contrast, modern AI systems learn their own internal mathematical
representations using gradient descent and high-dimensional optimisation. These models are sometimes
surprisingly good at spotting tumours or tissue changes that humans miss, although they can also behave oddly
when the input quality changes. Many hospitals are trialling these systems already, and even though they’re not
perfect, they could become almost essential as populations grow older and the demand for quick diagnosis gets
higher. Probability and statistics play a big role here because they help explain how confident the model is and
whether its decision can be trusted in borderline cases.

Another growing area is personalised medicine, where treatments are chosen based on a patient’s genetics or
medical history. The datasets in this area are huge and messy, and AI can help recognise patterns using
clustering or dimensionality-reduction techniques. But this only works if the mathematics underneath is solid,
because otherwise the patterns we think we’ve found might disappear as soon as new data are added. This is a
real issue in modern healthcare because doctors can’t depend on unstable predictions.

For future generations, especially students going into engineering, computing or medicine, understanding the
maths behind AI might end up being as important as traditional digital skills. If people know even the basics of
how these systems work, they’re less likely to treat AI as a mysterious black box and more likely to question it
when something doesn’t look right. The aim of the next section is to look at how these ideas play out in a more
practical, simplified example, and to think about what this might mean for the future of medical decision
making.

Analysis and discussion

When thinking about how maths and AI can actually change medical practice, it helps to look at a simple, but
realistic example. In medicine, especially in screening and early-diagnosis programmes, hospitals often have to
choose between different tools or strategies. Some are cheap and quick but not very accurate. Others are more
advanced, usually AI-assisted, and cost more upfront. This feels similar to engineering decisions where the
cheapest option is not always the smartest long-term choice.

Imagine a hospital choosing between different AI-supported screening systems for a particular cancer. One
system is cheap and uses a smaller model that is reasonably fast but not very sensitive. It misses some early-
stage problems, which means patients return later needing more aggressive treatment. A second option costs
more but uses a better trained model on a wider and more diverse dataset. It picks up subtle abnormalities and
gives more reliable predictions. A third, very advanced option might exist as well, but is so expensive that it
would eat most of the available budget. Table 1 shows a simple, made-up comparison between five possible AI
screening strategies, where higher cost generally brings better early detection.

If we only look at the purchase price, the cheapest option always wins. However, even a basic mathematical
model that includes probabilities, expected costs and a time horizon of several years tells a different story. A
cheap system that misses early cases produces hidden costs: extra treatments later, longer hospital stays and, in
some cases, worse outcomes. A mid-range system that seems too expensive at first can actually minimise the
cost per successful diagnosis once these future expenses are included. This kind of life-cycle style thinking is
familiar in engineering, but here the “assets” are patients rather than structures.

This is where AI and mathematics genuinely work together. AI provides predictions about who is likely to have
a particular condition; mathematics turns those predictions into decisions by combining them with costs, risks
and limits on resources. Probability distributions help estimate uncertainty, optimisation can suggest good
screening schedules, and relatively simple algebra is enough to compare strategies over time.

For future generations of students and doctors, the important skill will not just be using AI tools but questioning
them. Real hospital data are messy, incomplete and sometimes biased. A basic understanding of statistics and
modelling makes it easier to see when an apparently “smart” system is giving suspiciously confident answers or
behaving strangely on new patients. AI is unlikely to replace human judgement, but it can support it, as long as
both the mathematical reasoning and the limitations are understood honestly.
