Abstract

This paper looks at the relationship between mathematics and artificial intelligence, mainly in the context 
of modern medicine. Even though people often see AI as something mysterious, much of it is actually grounded 
in maths, especially optimisation, probability and geometry. After outlining some of the fundamental ideas, 
the paper uses a simplified medical screening example to show how maths and AI come together when making 
practical decisions. The aim is to highlight both the benefits and the limitations, which future generations 
will need to deal with carefully.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has developed so rapidly that sometimes people forget how reliant it still is on basic 
mathematical ideas. Even highly advanced systems used in hospitals, such as tumour-detection networks or 
prediction models, are based on things like optimisation, probability and linear algebra (Esteva et al., 2019). 
These mathematical structures remain important for checking reliability, bias and overall performance, especially 
in medicine where mistakes carry serious consequences.

Medical imaging is often one of the first examples discussed when talking about AI in healthcare. Older systems 
depended on fixed mathematical methods, but modern deep learning models learn patterns from thousands of images. 
They can be surprisingly effective at spotting tumours, although not perfect, and sometimes behave strangely when 
image quality varies (Crossnohere et al., 2022). Probability and uncertainty estimates help doctors understand how 
trustworthy these predictions actually are.

Another growing area is personalised medicine, where treatments are tailored to individual patients. The datasets 
involved can be huge and messy. AI helps reduce complexity and find patterns using clustering and dimensionality-
reduction methods. But these techniques are only reliable when the maths underneath is stable, otherwise misleading 
patterns can appear (Rajpurkar et al., 2022). This can be worrying for doctors who rely on accurate information to 
make quick decisions.

AI is spreading fast, and younger generations will encounter it everywhere, not just in engineering or computing 
courses. Understanding the maths behind AI might become almost as important as learning how to use a computer. 
Even a basic grasp of statistics helps people recognise when a model might be overconfident or behaving oddly. 
It also encourages a healthier scepticism, rather than blind trust in whatever output an AI provides.

Although AI is powerful, it is not magic, and part of the challenge for future medical professionals will be 
learning when to trust an AI tool and when to double-check things manually. The next section applies some of 
these ideas to a simple medical screening example, where both mathematics and AI play important roles.

In lectures and popular media, AI is sometimes described as if it were completely autonomous, but in reality it 
is still constrained by the equations and assumptions chosen by humans. Loss functions, learning rates and 
regularisation terms are all mathematical choices that influence how a system behaves in practice. Remembering 
this helps keep expectations realistic and reminds us that AI tools should be tuned and checked, not simply 
trusted because they sound impressive.

Analysis and discussion

To see how maths and AI work together in real situations, consider a simplified medical screening problem. Many 
hospitals need to choose between several diagnostic tools. Cheaper systems cost less upfront, but may fail to detect 
early-stage disease. More advanced AI systems usually perform better but can be expensive and require specialised 
equipment (Topol, 2019). In practice, hospitals often have to balance performance with budget limitations, which is 
not always as straightforward as it sounds.

Imagine a hospital comparing five AI-supported screening options. A low-cost model detects around half of early 
cases, while more advanced models detect a significantly higher percentage. Table 1 below includes fictional but

If decision-makers only look at upfront cost, the cheapest strategy seems the obvious choice. However, once we use a 
basic mathematical cost–benefit model—including probabilities of missed diagnoses, projected long-term treatment 
costs, and the chance of detecting cases earlier—the cheapest option is often the worst. Missing early cancers leads 
to more expensive treatments later, and in many cases, poorer patient outcomes overall. Sometimes the mid-range 
system gives the best balance between cost and performance, even though it looks more expensive at first 
(Chustecki, 2024).

This is where the relationship between maths and AI becomes clearer. AI provides predictions, such as probability 
scores or confidence intervals, but mathematics is needed to interpret these predictions correctly. Tools like 
optimisation models, basic algebra and probability distributions allow hospitals to compare strategies in a fair and 
structured way. In other words, the AI gives the numbers, but mathematics helps reveal what those numbers actually 
mean in practice.

For future generations relying heavily on AI in medicine, it will be important not just to use these systems but to 
question them. Sometimes AI models behave unpredictably when presented with new kinds of data, and without even a 
basic understanding of statistics, it can be easy to miss warning signs. Being able to recognise when an AI model is 
being “too confident” or when its predictions don’t match clinical reality is a skill that will matter more and more. 
Even small mathematical insights—like understanding what probability actually means—can make someone a better 
decision-maker.

AI will not completely replace doctors or engineers, but when combined with sound mathematical reasoning, it can 
help support better decisions, reduce errors, and improve patient outcomes.

There is also a wider ethical question about who gets access to the best models. If only wealthier hospitals can 
afford high-performing AI tools, then mathematical improvements could actually widen health inequalities rather 
than reduce them. Being aware of these social effects is part of using maths responsibly, not just solving 
equations on paper. Future engineers and clinicians will have to keep this bigger picture in mind when they 
design or approve new AI systems.
